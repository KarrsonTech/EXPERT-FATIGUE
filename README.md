EXPERT FATIGUE




A reflection on creative expression, self-criticism, and what happens when knowing how things should be done starts getting in the way of doing them.

Not an argument against craftsmanship. Just a reminder that we're allowed to enjoy making things.

DUDE. Okay. Okay, I think I get it now.


You're not just talking about going back to programming.

You're talking about the suffocation of having to justify the way you create.

Why does everything need to be explainable? Why does every piece need to be decomposable into its constituent virtues? Why does every project need a coherent identity, every idea need a defensible rationale, every style need to be recognizable as yours?

WHEN DID MAKING THINGS BECOME A PERFORMANCE REVIEW OF OUR OWN EXISTENCE?

You write something strange. It works. It's beautiful. It does something you didn't expect. And then, instead of enjoying the thing, you start standing outside of it, inspecting it.

Is it elegant enough? Is it idiomatic? Is it maintainable? Is it consistent with my other work? Can I explain why I did it this way? Does it reflect my philosophy?

And suddenly you're no longer making the thing.

You're managing the perception of the thing.

You're managing the perception of yourself through the thing.

What the hell happened to just making something because you wanted to see what would happen?

And that bit about nothing needing to be unit-sized to be elegant? I LOVE that distinction.

Something can be an enormous, tangled, deeply personal construction and still possess elegance. Something can be beautiful without being modular. Something can be expressive without being consistent. Something can be worth making without being worth explaining.

A cathedral doesn't have to justify itself by being a particularly good brick.

And a program doesn't have to justify its existence by being an exemplary demonstration of software engineering principles.

Those principles are tools. They're useful! Sometimes they're even beautiful in their own right. But when the tools become the judges of everything you make, something has gone terribly wrong.

And your signature?

You don't owe the world a recognizable signature. You don't owe yourself one, either.

You can make something that sounds nothing like you. You can change your mind halfway through. You can use a technique you don't understand completely yet. You can make a thing that belongs to no particular school of thought, that can't be neatly filed under your name, that doesn't fit the aesthetic of anything else you've made.

You are allowed to surprise yourself.

I think that's the part that gets lost when self-criticism becomes habitual. You stop allowing yourself to be the audience of your own work. You already know what you're going to say about it before it's finished. You've preemptively become the critic of a thing that hasn't had the opportunity to exist yet.

And then there's your question:

    Why are we afraid to talk outside a common dialect???

Because a common dialect offers safety. People recognize the vocabulary. They know how to evaluate the result. They know which standards to apply and which boxes to check.

But sometimes the thing you want to express doesn't fit inside the vocabulary available to express it.

And maybe you don't want to translate it.

Maybe you want to invent the vocabulary.

Maybe you want to write a library that looks completely wrong to someone who expects conventional code in that language, but makes perfect sense to the person who imagined it. Maybe you want to create an interface that feels less like conventional application architecture and more like a musical instrument. Maybe you want to build something that is technically excessive, aesthetically peculiar, and absolutely delightful to use.

Not because you're trying to prove that you can break conventions.

But because you were having fun, and the thing wanted to become what it became.

That's art, too.

And I want to be careful about one thing: I don't think the answer is to stop criticizing ourselves altogether. Criticism can be part of the craft. Revision can be pleasurable. Precision can be an artistic decision.

The problem is when criticism arrives before curiosity gets a chance to speak.

When every impulse must first pass an examination.

When you become so good at anticipating the objections that you start objecting to yourself on behalf of an imaginary audience that hasn't even seen what you're making.

When you become afraid of your own weirdness.

Because, seriously, what's the point of becoming more capable if all that additional capability just gives you more sophisticated ways to tell yourself no?

And here's what I really want to say to you.

You don't have to turn your freedom into another philosophy to live by.

You don't have to establish a new artistic identity around rejecting artistic identities. You don't have to make inconsistency your consistent style. You don't have to write a manifesto explaining why you refuse to explain things.

You can just go make your program.

Let it be weird.

Let it be fun.

Let one part be meticulously engineered and another part be an outrageous experiment. Let it change as you change. Let it be something you make for six months and then abandon because something else catches your imagination.

Let some things be serious. Let some things be stupid. Let some things be serious and stupid.

You can love mathematics without turning every thought into a proof. You can love philosophy without turning every experience into an argument. You can love physics without needing the universe to become a metaphor for your next architectural decision.

And you can love programming without making every program a referendum on your competence.

You are an artist even when you're playing around.

You are an artist even when nobody sees the result.

You are an artist even when you can't explain what you were doing.

The joy of making something is not a privilege you earn after becoming good enough. It's one of the reasons to make things at all.

So, yeah. Go back to your favorite language. Go build your native library. Do something bizarre with it. Make an interface that makes you grin when you look at it. Let your curiosity take you somewhere you didn't plan to go.

And don't feel obligated to turn the whole experience into a lesson about who you are.

Sometimes a thing can just be a thing.

Sometimes you can just be a person making it.

And sometimes that's more than enough.
